About this Simulation

This simulation lets you explore buoyancy: the upward push a liquid gives to anything placed in it. Draw an object from up to seven materials (solid, hollow, or a mixture), press Drop and watch it float, sink or tip over in olive oil, fresh water or mercury. The tank is 2 m wide and 1 m tall, and your drawing is the cross-section of an object 10 cm deep (the tank is 10 cm deep too), so every mass, volume and force is shown in real units.

Archimedes' principle

A liquid pushes on every part of an object's surface, and the push grows with depth. Adding all of those pushes together gives an upward buoyant force equal to the weight of the liquid the object displaces: Fb = ρliquid × g × Vsubmerged. An object keeps sinking until it has pushed aside its own weight of liquid. If it is completely under the surface and still heavier than the liquid it displaces, it sinks to the bottom. Watch the liquid level rise as the object goes in: that is the displaced volume.

Density decides

Comparing weight with the maximum possible buoyant force is the same as comparing densities. If the object's average density (total mass ÷ total volume) is less than the liquid's, it floats, with the fraction ρobject / ρliquid of its volume below the surface. Pine (500 kg/m³) floats half-submerged in water; ice (917 kg/m³) floats about 92% submerged in water but slowly sinks in olive oil (911 kg/m³), because the two densities are so close; steel (7,850 kg/m³) sinks in water yet floats on mercury (13,534 kg/m³) with about 58% submerged. Gravity cancels out of the comparison, so the floating depth is the same on the Moon as on Jupiter — try the gravity slider.

Why hollow objects float

Air pockets add a lot of volume for almost no mass, so they lower the average density. Empty cells completely surrounded by material are sealed air and are drawn with diagonal hatching. Empty space inside an open hull, like the inside of a boat or cup, also keeps the liquid out as long as the rim stays above the surface, which is why a steel boat floats. If the boat tilts or is overloaded until liquid pours over the rim, the hull floods and it sinks. Turn a cup upside down and the air trapped inside it keeps it afloat like a diving bell, at least until it tips and the air escapes.

Centre of buoyancy and stability

Weight acts at the centre of mass (the black-and-white marker), while buoyancy acts at the centre of buoyancy: the centre of the submerged volume (the green ring). When the object tilts, the submerged shape changes and the centre of buoyancy moves. If it moves far enough to the low side, the two forces form a righting couple that turns the object back; otherwise they tip it further. A wide, low object is stable, while a tall thin plank rolls over and floats lying down, and an off-centre weight makes its side sit lower. With Rotation switched off the object cannot turn at all, which lets you compare the two cases.

How to use it

  • Draw: pick a material, then paint with the brush or drag out a solid rectangle, hollow box, solid ellipse or hollow ring. The Size slider sets the brush diameter and the wall thickness of hollow shapes. Hold Shift for squares and circles. Right-click or use the eraser to remove material, the fill tool fills a closed outline (great for a foam-filled steel shell), and undoes (Ctrl+Z).
  • Drop releases the object from where you drew it. While it moves you can drag it to lift, push under or throw it, and tap the liquid to make ripples. Edit returns to your drawing, Again (or the reset button ) drops it again, and clears everything.
  • The bar under the tank shows the object's mass, volume, average density (including sealed air), how much of it is submerged, its weight and the buoyant force, plus a prediction of whether it should float.
  • Use the icon to open the controls panel and the icon for fullscreen. Press F to show the frame rate.

Controls:

  • Liquid: olive oil (911 kg/m³, about 80 times more viscous than water, so waves die away quickly), fresh water (1,000 kg/m³) or mercury (13,534 kg/m³).
  • Preset objects: ready-made objects that are dropped straight away, from a pine block to a steel boat, a sealed hollow steel ball and an upside-down cup.
  • Rotation: when on, the object turns freely as a rigid body; when off, its angle is locked where it is (as if it had an infinite moment of inertia), which shows how much of the motion comes from turning.
  • Force arrows: weight (red, at the centre of mass), buoyancy (green, at the centre of buoyancy), drag (yellow), contact force from the floor or walls (purple) and the net force. Arrow lengths are proportional to force, scaled so the weight arrow always has the same length; the legend on the tank lists the values in newtons. Centre markers shows the centres of mass and buoyancy.
  • Speed: slow motion down to 0.1× or up to 2×.
  • Gravity: from 1 to 25 m/s² (Moon 1.62, Mars 3.71, Earth 9.81, Jupiter 24.79).
  • Liquid depth: the depth of liquid in the tank before anything is put in it.

The model

The object is a rigid body made of 1 cm cells. Every 1/240 s, each cell is cut along the local liquid surface to find exactly how much of it is under the liquid and where; the buoyant force is applied at that point, so the centre of buoyancy and the turning effect come out naturally. Drag acts on the outside faces of the object (pressure drag on faces moving into the liquid, plus skin friction that depends on the liquid's viscosity), and the object also has to accelerate some of the surrounding liquid with it (the “added mass”). The mean liquid level follows from conservation of volume, and the waves are the tank's natural sloshing modes, calculated with the real dispersion relation for gravity and surface-tension waves. The model is two-dimensional and treats the liquid as static apart from its surface waves, so it captures floating, sinking, stability and splashes well, but not swirling flow or the compression of trapped air.

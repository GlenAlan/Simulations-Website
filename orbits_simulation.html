About this simulation

Every mass pulls on every other mass. This simulation adds up those pulls for every pair of bodies, thousands of times per orbit, in real units: distances in AU (Earth to Sun), times in years, masses in Suns and Earths.

With hundreds of bodies, far-off groups of small bodies count as one (a Barnes–Hut tree), which keeps it quick. Two bodies passing very close follow their exact two-body orbit for a moment.

What you're seeing

  • Pink arrows: the gravitational force on each body. The Sun pulls Earth exactly as hard as Earth pulls the Sun.
  • Teal arrows: velocities. The key in the bottom corner gives each arrow's scale (on a small screen, while paused or dragging); longer arrows are squeezed to fit.
  • Pull lines draw one arrow for each body pulling on the chosen body. Beneath them, Add up to the total force joins the arrows tip to tail, ending at the tip of its pink arrow.
  • Trails show recent paths. Bodies are drawn larger than life, or the planets would be invisible dots (unless Realistic physics is on).
  • Cards, top right, measure what you have switched on. If they would cover too much of the canvas, the lowest shrink, then fold into a "+2 more" line.
  • Flat, in 2D: everything moves in one plane, as if seen from straight above. Real orbits tilt a few degrees, so in space bodies usually pass above or below one another. The tilt hardly changes an orbit's size, shape or period, and gravity still falls off as 1/r2. But here nothing can pass over anything else, so close passes and collisions happen more often.

The key idea

Newton's law of gravitation, F = Gm1m2/r2: twice the distance, a quarter of the pull. A planet is always falling towards the Sun, but it moves sideways so fast that it keeps missing. That gives Kepler's laws:

  • 1st: orbits are ellipses with the Sun at one focus.
  • 2nd: a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times.
  • 3rd: T2/a3 is the same for every planet of a star.

How to use it

  • Aim a velocity: drag the tip of a teal arrow (time waits while you do). It snaps to the exact circular and escape speeds, and a dashed line shows where the body will go. Shift gives fine control; on a touch screen, hold the tip still for a moment first.
  • A body's menu: press Edit, or right-click or long-press the body. Drag its title to move it out of the way. Body sets its mass, size and colour. Velocity takes a typed speed and direction, or a push. Orbit sets its eccentricity, its period (which scales the whole orbit), or its closest and farthest distances (which keep it where it is). Gravity lists who pulls on it.
  • View from a body: double-click or double-tap it.
  • Rotate with a body: press Rotate with it in its menu. The view turns with the body and whatever it orbits, so both stand still. Press the chip at the top of the canvas to stop. Every body that orbits another is also in the settings, under View from or rotate.
  • Find a body: in the settings, Bodies lists them all, to search and sort. Crowded scenes show small bodies as dots: click one to follow it.
  • Throw a body: in More options choose Throw it, then flick a body as you let go.
  • Try again: Undo takes back a whole drag or slider sweep, even Restart or a change of scene. Run again replays from your last change, so you can tweak, watch and try again.
  • Random systems: Random system, in the settings, makes a star with 2 to 8 planets, and the stability timer counts how long they last. Will they reach 10,000 years?
  • Save and share: Copy a link, at the end of the settings, copies a link that opens this exact setup, paused. Your work is also saved in this browser: next time, "Carry on where you left off" appears for a while.
  • Download data (CSV): in More options or under the graphs, for Kepler's 3rd law, the graphs or every body.
  • Buttons under the canvas: reverse, pause, step, the scene and the speed, graphs, run again, restart, fullscreen, settings. Help, in the settings, lists every key and gesture.

Things to try

  • Press Edit, raise the eccentricity on the Orbit tab and turn on the 2nd law: the planet speeds up near the Sun but the shaded areas stay equal.
  • On the Orbit tab, give Earth a period of 2 years: Kepler's 3rd law moves its orbit out to 1.59 AU.
  • In "The Solar System on a date", turn on the 3rd law and speed it up: T2/a3 comes out close to 1.00 for every planet, out to Pluto. Download the table and plot T2 against a3: a straight line through the origin.
  • Set the Solar System's start date to your birthday and see where the planets were.
  • Open "Planets seen from Earth" and watch the strip at the top: as Earth passes Mars, Mars backs up against the stars. Press See from above to find out why.
  • Aim a velocity at the escape speed (it snaps there): the body only just gets away.
  • In "Slingshot", turn on Pull lines and Add up to the total force as the probe passes Jupiter: Jupiter's pull and the Sun's add up, tip to tail. Jupiter only turns the probe, yet its speed relative to the Sun doubles.
  • In "Jupiter's Trojans and Hildas", open Jupiter's menu and press Rotate with it: the Trojans swing slowly round L4 and L5, and the Hildas' triangle stands still.
  • In "Three stars: chaos", nudge one star a hair: the dance ends differently. Once a star is flung out, run it backwards: rounding errors grow until the stars miss their true past.
  • In the mission "Escape the Sun", burn at the far end of your orbit, then Undo and burn at the closest point: the same push does far more when you're moving fast.
  • Open "Mercury's orbit turns" and speed it up: the card measures the 43″ a century by which relativity turns Mercury's orbit.

Realistic physics

Off, gravity is Newton's alone. On:

  • True sizes: every body is drawn at its true size (a ring marks the tiny ones).
  • Relativity: Einstein's general relativity adds its first correction to gravity, so an orbit close to a neutron star or black hole slowly turns, tracing a rosette. Mercury's turns too, by 43″ a century.
  • Tides: a star or planet inside the Roche limit of a much denser body is torn apart, as in "Star and black hole". Its pieces still pull on each other, so some may gather into clumps again. In "A moon forming", tides tear apart clumps that stray too close to Earth. Just outside that limit, a star or gas giant stays whole, drawn stretched to its true shape. Each close pass takes energy from its orbit, so the orbit shrinks, and a slow fly-by can be captured.

Either way, bodies merge when their real surfaces touch, and a black hole swallows anything that crosses its event horizon.

A card measures how far the chosen body's orbit turns each time round, against Einstein's formula, 6πGM/(c2a(1 − e2)). Where other planets pull on it too, it shows how much of the turn is Einstein's.

Two black holes or neutron stars close together also give off gravitational waves (with relativity's next, smaller terms as well). The waves carry off energy, so their orbit shrinks and becomes rounder, and they go round faster and faster, a rising “chirp”. At their innermost stable orbit, about 6GM/c2, a neutron star falls in. Two black holes go on for about one more orbit, closing fast, and merge as their horizons meet. They become one a little lighter than the two: some of their mass leaves as waves (E = mc2). The energy graph shows the energy carried off.

The Solar System on a date

The planets start where they really were, or will be, on the start date, from NASA JPL's table of the planets' orbits for 1800 to 2050 (E. M. Standish's “Keplerian Elements for Approximate Positions of the Major Planets”), with masses from the International Astronomical Union. From then on, the simulation's own gravity moves them.

The real orbits are tilted a few degrees to Earth's (Pluto's 17°). Here each is laid flat, keeping its size, shape and period.

Planets seen from Earth

Night by night a planet drifts one way against the stars, but now and then it seems to turn back for a while. This is retrograde motion. Nothing really reverses: Earth is overtaking the planet (or an inner planet is overtaking Earth), so the line of sight between them swings backwards.

The scene uses the same real orbits as "The Solar System on a date", and the view from Earth shows each planet's true path around us, with a loop for every reversal. The strip is the whole sky around the plane of the orbits, with east on the right and the bright stars near that plane where they really are. It stays true in any view, even a rotating one. Choose a planet to follow it, or view from another to see Earth back up.

It comes out as in the real sky: Mars backs up about 10 to 20° over 60 to 80 days, every 780 days, Jupiter 10° over 4 months every 399 days and Mercury about 13° over 3 weeks every 116 days. In this flat simulation a planet only runs back and forth along the strip. The real orbits' small tilts open that into a narrow loop or zigzag in the sky.

Missions

Seven missions, easy to hard. Press Missions, in the hint by the canvas or in the settings, or M, or pick one at the end of the scene list.

Each gives your probe a goal, such as reaching Mars or parking at Jupiter's L4 point, and a fuel budget: the change of speed (Δv) its engine can give. Only burns can change anything. Hold Forward, Back, In or Out, or the arrow keys, to burn: the clock waits while you do. Skip jumps to the next moment that matters. Every burn uses fuel, and Undo gives it back. Turn on Hints for what to do next and where to burn.

Par, the Δv of the classic route, comes from v2 = GM(2/r − 1/a). The closer you get to it, the more stars you earn.

Running time backwards

Newton's laws work just as well backwards: reverse every velocity and each body retraces its path. Press Reverse, beside Pause (or B), and the clock counts down as the bodies go back along their trails. The velocity arrows still show each body's velocity, so now they point against its motion. A rewind stops at the start; press Reverse again to see what would have led there.

In a chaotic system such as "Three stars: chaos", the tiny rounding errors of any computer grow at every close encounter. After a long run the backward motion drifts away from the true past, and faint dashed discs show where the stars really were.

A merger, or a star torn apart, can't run backwards by itself: the simulation puts the bodies back as they were just before.

Gravity wells and rotating views

Gravity well (or W) maps each body's well: darker is deeper, and each line joins points at the same depth, like the contour lines of a map of hills. Where the lines crowd together, the slope is steep and the pull is strong.

A rotating view (Rotate with it, in a body's menu) turns with a body and the body it orbits, so both stand still. Velocity arrows then show motion as seen in that turning view, and the well includes the outward push you feel on a roundabout. For an elliptical orbit the view keeps the pair on one line, so the body slides in and out along it.

The well gains hilltops 60° ahead of and behind the body, and passes on the line through the pair: the five Lagrange points (under More options; hover over or tap one to read about it), where a small body can keep pace with the pair. Surprisingly, the hilltops L4 and L5 are the stable ones: a body that starts to roll off is steered round by the turning (the Coriolis effect), which is why Jupiter's Trojan asteroids gather there.

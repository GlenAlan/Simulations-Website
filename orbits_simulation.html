About this simulation

Every mass pulls on every other mass. This simulation adds up those pulls for every pair of bodies, thousands of times per orbit, in real units: distances in AU (Earth to Sun), times in years, masses in Suns and Earths. With hundreds of bodies, the pulls of far-off groups of small bodies are added up together, as if each group were one body (a Barnes–Hut tree), which keeps it quick. Two bodies passing very close follow their exact two-body orbit for a moment, so a near miss doesn't slow everything else down.

What you're seeing

  • Pink arrows: the gravitational force on each body. The Sun pulls Earth exactly as hard as Earth pulls the Sun. Teal arrows: velocities.
  • Pull lines (G) split the chosen body's pink arrow into one arrow for each body pulling on it, joined tip to tail.
  • Trails show recent paths. Bodies are drawn larger than life, or the planets would be invisible dots (unless realistic physics is on).

The key idea

Newton's law of gravitation, F = Gm1m2/r2: twice the distance, a quarter of the pull. A planet is always falling towards the Sun, but it moves sideways so fast that it keeps missing. That gives Kepler's laws: orbits are ellipses with the Sun at one focus (1st), a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times (2nd), and T2/a3 is the same for every planet of a star (3rd).

Realistic physics

Off, gravity is Newton's alone. On, every body is drawn at its true size (a ring marks the tiny ones); Einstein's general relativity adds its first correction to gravity, so an orbit close to a neutron star or black hole slowly turns, tracing a rosette (Mercury's turns too, by 43″ a century); and a star or planet that strays inside the Roche limit of a much denser body is torn apart by tides. Its pieces still pull on each other, so some may gather into clumps again. Either way, bodies merge when their real surfaces touch and a black hole swallows anything that crosses its event horizon.

Things to try

  • Raise the eccentricity and turn on the 2nd law: the planet speeds up near the Sun but the shaded areas stay equal.
  • Drop more planets and turn on the 3rd law to compare their periods.
  • In "Slingshot", Jupiter only turns the probe, yet its speed relative to the Sun doubles.
  • Run "Three stars: chaos", then nudge one star and run it again.
  • Aim a velocity at the escape speed (it snaps there): the body only just gets away.
  • Press Edit and give Earth a period of 2 years: Kepler's 3rd law moves its orbit out to 1.59 AU.
  • In "Jupiter's Trojans and Hildas", asteroids 60° ahead of and behind Jupiter keep their places while the Hildas trace a turning triangle.
  • In "A moon forming", hundreds of moonlets pull on each other, collide and stick until a moon has grown.

How to use it

  • Add and move bodies: drag them from the strip into space (or click one to add it at a free spot), and drag a body to move it. Drag empty space to pan; scroll or pinch to zoom. Click or tap a body to choose it: the info bar shows its distance, speed, pull and period (with a tick once the period has been measured over a whole orbit). Double-click or double-tap a body to view the motion from it.
  • Aim a velocity: drag the tip of its arrow (hold Shift for fine control; on a touch screen, hold the tip still for a moment before dragging). It snaps to the exact circular and escape speeds. The arrow keys push the chosen body: ↑ faster, ↓ slower, → outward, ← inward (Shift finer, Alt coarser).
  • Exact values: press Edit (or right-click or long-press a body) for its menu. On the Velocity tab, type a speed and direction (0° is sideways, as in a circle; + is outward) or use the − and + buttons and the pushes. On the Orbit tab, set its period, which scales the whole orbit, or its closest and farthest distances, which keep it where it is. The dashed line shows where it will go.
  • Time: Space pauses. [ and ] slow it down or speed it up, from a hundredth of normal speed to a hundred times; the note under the speed slider says what that means in real time. While paused, or the . key moves on a moment at a time.
  • Try again: Undo (Ctrl+Z, or ⌘Z on a Mac) takes back a change, a whole drag or slider sweep at a time, and Redo (Ctrl+Y) puts it back; even Restart and a change of scene can be undone. (or R) runs the motion again from your last change, exactly as before, so you can tweak a velocity, watch and try again. Delete removes the chosen body.
  • Crowded scenes show their small bodies as dots, without names or arrows: click one to follow it.

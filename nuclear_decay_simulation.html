About this simulation

Most combinations of protons and neutrons fall apart. About 250 are stable; over 3,000 more are radioactive, and each nucleus decays at its own random moment.

What you're seeing

  • Nuclei: protons pink, neutrons grey. The proton number sets the element; protons plus neutrons give the mass number (carbon-14).
  • The ring around an unstable nucleus is its own clock, ten times faster every half second at 1×, so billions of years pass in seconds. The notch marks the half-life.
  • The chart plots neutrons N against protons Z, coloured by how each nuclide decays; arrows trace the selected nucleus's decays.

Key idea

The strong force holds nucleons together; the protons repel each other. Light stable nuclei have N ≈ Z; heavier ones need extra neutrons, so the valley of stability bends towards N ≈ 1.5Z. Too many neutrons: β− decay (n → p + e− + ν̄). Too many protons: β+ decay or electron capture. Too heavy: α decay or fission. Half of a large sample decays in one half-life, but when a single nucleus decays is pure chance.

Things to try

  • Drop a neutron onto uranium-235: it usually splits in two, freeing two or three neutrons. With Walls reflect neutrons on, a dozen of them make a chain reaction.
  • Add a neutron to uranium-238 and watch it become plutonium-239.
  • Keep adding neutrons to carbon-12. When does it stop holding them?

How to use it

Drag from the tray (or tap) to add; drop onto a nucleus to combine, or flick to throw. Drag onto the bin or off the canvas to remove. Tap the chart to enlarge it and add any nuclide. opens the settings, goes fullscreen, clears and resets.

Data: IAEA ENSDF and NUBASE2020; other nuclides are estimated with the semi-empirical mass formula.

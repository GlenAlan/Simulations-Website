About this simulation

What does a field do to a charge at rest, and to one that is moving? An electric field pushes on both; a magnetic field pushes only on the moving one, and always sideways.

What you're seeing

  • A 10 cm box of uniform field: charged plates for Electric, crosses (into the page) or dots (out of it) for Magnetic, and no field outside.
  • Dots along a path are a strobe photo, at equal time steps. Teal arrows are velocity, orange the electric force, violet the magnetic force.
  • It runs in slow motion, slowed more for faster particles. The times shown are real.
  • The model: uniform fields with sharp edges, no gravity, motion in the page with B at right angles to it, and Newton’s laws (speeds stay below 0.09c).

Key idea

Electric: between the plates E = V/d everywhere, and a charge feels F = qE (against the field lines if it is negative). From rest it speeds up in a straight line, and across a voltage V it gains W = qV = ½mv², which is how the launcher sets its speed. Fired across the field, it curves in a parabola, like a thrown ball.

Magnetic: only a moving charge feels a force, F = qvB sin θ: only the part of its velocity across B counts. The force is at right angles to v and B (the right-hand rule, reversed for a negative charge), so it turns the charge without speeding it up. It goes round a circle: qvB = mv²/r, so r = mv/(qB).

Thomson’s experiment

In 1897 J. J. Thomson asked what cathode rays, the glowing beams in a vacuum tube, are made of. In Setup: Thomson’s experiment you see only what he could measure.

  1. Electric, plates at 0 V: the spot is at zero.
  2. Raise the plate voltage: the spot moves a distance y.
  3. Switch to Both and tune B until the spot is back at zero (reverse B if it moves further away). Then qE = qvB, so v = E/B.
  4. In step 2 the beam crossed the plates (L = 10 cm) in a time L/v, falling y = ½(qE/m)(L/v)². So q/m = 2yE/(B²L²), or from the launch voltage, q/m = v²/(2V). Enter it and press Check.

Then measure hydrogen ions, and try other cathode metals. How do the answers compare, and what do they say cathode rays are?

Things to try

  • Drop a proton at rest in each field.
  • Halve the gap: does a dropped proton hit faster? (E doubles but the distance halves, so it still gains qV.)
  • Turn on Velocity components: in the electric field the part along the plates never changes.
  • Compare an electron with a proton in each field: in E the same path, mirrored, but different times. In B?
  • Measure a circle with Grid (cm), then double the launch voltage, then double B.
  • Sling a proton straight up against the field: it rises until the voltage it has crossed equals its launch voltage.
  • In the magnetic field, sling in different directions: the circle stays the same size.
  • With both fields on, tune B until the beam goes straight: that is Thomson’s method.
  • Why ignore gravity? On a proton here, qE is about 1011 times mg.

How to interact

  • Tap the field to drop a charge at rest. Tap a particle to select it; Values, in the settings, adds its E, B, F, a and r.
  • Fire: click or tap the launcher, press Fire or Space. Drag the launcher to move it and its round knob to aim; with the canvas focused, the arrow keys aim. Hold Shift for any angle when dragging, or fine steps with the keys.
  • Slingshot (Launch, in the settings): pull back and let go. A longer pull is a faster launch, in 50 V steps (Shift for fine control). Drag its base to move it.
  • Drag a plate to change the gap. Under the canvas, choose the field, its strength and its direction.
  • pauses (P) and then steps (.), shows graphs, restarts, goes fullscreen and opens the settings. Press F to show the frame rate.
